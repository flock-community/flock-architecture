# Specified contracts

Every system that other people use has a contract: an agreement about what goes over the wire, what you can ask, and what comes back, in which shape. If nobody wrote it down, the contract is whatever your system happens to do today.

A specified contract is the same agreement, but explicit and looked after:

1. **Write it down.** A specification describes everything that crosses the edge of your system, for machines to check and people to discuss.
2. **Keep it thin.** It contains what the other side needs, and nothing about how the inside works.
3. **Change it in versions.** A change is a decision with a version number and an announcement, and an old version is retired on a date.

## The contract you already have

Take the web shop from the [overview](/). The frontend and a partner's website both show customers where their parcel is. Both get it from Shipping, and neither can look inside. They see its **interface**: what can be called, and what comes back. Nobody signed anything, but that is a contract. Shipping, the **provider**, offers data in a certain shape, and the **consumers** on the other side expect it to stay that way.

While the agreement is implicit, a consumer builds on everything it sees, because nothing says which parts are a promise and which are there by accident. The provider has no idea what people rely on. It either changes things and finds out afterwards who broke, or it stops changing things.

A contract belongs at the edge: wherever the two sides are built and released separately, whether what crosses it is a web request, a message on a queue or a file. And nothing should be reachable that isn't in it, least of all the database. [Domain isolation](/domain-isolation#contexts-do-not-overlap) rules that out for its own reasons.

## Write it down

A specification lists the **operations** a consumer can ask for, with what goes in and comes out, down to the type of every field and the errors that can come back. Here is part of Shipping's contract in OpenAPI, the most widely used language for web interfaces:

```yaml title="From Shipping's contract, in OpenAPI"
ShipmentResponse:
  type: object
  required: [id, status]
  properties:
    id:
      type: string
    status:
      type: string
      enum: [PREPARING, IN_TRANSIT, DELIVERED]
    expectedDelivery:
      type: string
      format: date
```

In words: a shipment always has an identifier and one of three statuses, and may have an expected delivery date. Which language you use is a tooling decision. That you use one is the architectural decision.

Such a document is independent of any programming language, so the other team and the business can read it and negotiate about it. And a machine can read it, so the promise is checked instead of trusted: tools generate both sides' code from it, and two versions can be compared, so an unannounced breaking change is caught before it ships.

### Specified, not first

*Contract first* means you write the specification and generate code from it. *Code first* means you write the code and derive the specification from it. We deliberately don't call this pillar "contract first", because the order is not the point. What matters is that a specification exists and is treated as the contract: kept under version control, reviewed as a change to a promise, and what you point consumers to. A specification generated on the fly from whatever the code does today is not a contract.

When you connect someone to an existing domain, negotiate the contract before any data flows, because whatever you share "just for now" becomes the contract.

## Keep it thin

An interface is thin. An implementation is heavy. And the two change at different speeds: behind the contract your team decides alone and can release many times a day, while at the edge every change involves other people with plans of their own. That is slow by nature, with or without a contract. Writing it down decides whether slow becomes stuck. When nobody knows what is relied on, nothing can be touched safely. With a contract you know exactly what you have to keep supporting. Everything else is free to change, as long as every build honours the contract, in shape and in [behaviour](#what-a-specification-cant-hold).

### Give away as little as you can

The quick way to make a first version is to turn the domain model outward and send each class over the wire as it is. At first this seems free. But every field you publish is a promise to people you may not know, for a period you don't control. You have to assume they use all of it, so the model can't change shape anymore. The inside has become as slow as the outside.

Adding to a contract later is easy. Taking something back is hard. So start small: ask the consumers for the least they can work with. Shipping's consumers asked where a parcel is and when it arrives, and that is what they got. Which carrier takes it stays inside, and where a shipment goes through seven steps inside, the contract promises three.

## Change it in versions

Versioning lets a contract change slowly instead of not at all.

### Promise more, require less

A **compatible** change leaves every consumer working. A **breaking** change forces at least one of them to do something. One rule tells you which is which: you may always promise more and require less. A new operation or a new field in an answer is compatible. A field that is removed, renamed or changes type is breaking, and so is a new required field or a stricter check. The rule only works if consumers ignore what they don't recognise, a habit called the **tolerant reader**.

### Two versions side by side

A breaking change is never made in place. You publish the new version next to the old one, and for a while both work. The old one is **deprecated**: it still works, but it is on its way out. Consumers move over when it suits their own planning, within a period you set.

<VersionsFigure />

A useful deprecation says what goes away, when, and what to use instead. Announcing is the easy half. Switching the old version off is the half that gets skipped, and without it interfaces pile up, each costing maintenance and each a door to guard. So save up breaking changes until together they are worth a new version. Events that have been published can't be switched off like an old version. [Event-driven](/event-driven) explains what that asks of their readers.

You can only retire what you can see, so know your consumers by name and count the calls per consumer, per version and per operation. Retiring a version is then a countdown instead of a guess.

## What a specification can't hold

A specification captures shapes, and consumers depend on behaviour just as much. Say Shipping's list of shipments has always come back in the order they were made, only because the database happened to return them that way, and the frontend shows the last three as "recent deliveries". Then Shipping moves to another database, and customers see parcels from years ago. No tool reports a breaking change, and something broke anyway.

This is Hyrum's Law: with enough consumers, every observable behaviour will be relied on by somebody. So if a behaviour matters, put it in the contract, for example as a sort parameter with a stated default. As a consumer, rely on nothing that isn't written. As a provider, treat a visible change in behaviour as a change to the contract.

## Conclusion

Every system in use has a contract. The only choice is whether you write it down. A specification describes everything that crosses the edge, in a form people can negotiate about and machines can check. Keep it thin, so that everything behind it can change fast. Promise more and require less. Anything else is a new version, and the old one retires on a date. And know who uses what, because you can only retire what you can see.

Specified contracts are the edge of the circle. [Domain isolation](/domain-isolation) is about what the edge protects, [Event-driven](/event-driven) about what travels from one edge to the next, and the [overview](/) shows how the three depend on each other.
