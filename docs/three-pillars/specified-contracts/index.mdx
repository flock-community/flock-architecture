# Specified contracts

Every system that others connect to has an **interface**: what can be asked of it, and what comes back. The system that offers it is the **producer**. Whatever connects to it, usually another system, is a **consumer**. Producer and consumer both rely on an agreement about what crosses the interface. That agreement is a **contract**, and it exists whether or not anyone has specified it. Left unspecified, it is whatever the system happens to do today.

A specified contract makes the agreement explicit. It lives in one central place, such as a code repository, where the producer, its consumers and anyone else involved can find it. People read it as documentation, and because it is structured, machines can generate code from it. It always carries a version, so everyone knows which agreement is in force.

## Implicit versus specified

Consider the web shop from the [overview](/). The shop's frontend, the part customers see in their browser, shows where their parcel is, and so does a partner's website. Both are consumers of Shipping, the producer. Nobody signed anything, yet both expect Shipping's answers to keep their shape.

While the contract remains implicit, Shipping is in effect exposing its whole domain, internals included. Nothing says which parts are a promise and which are visible by accident, so consumers build on everything they can reach. The partner's tracking page shows all seven internal steps of a shipment. What has been given away can no longer change freely.

The cost appears with the first change. Shipping merges two of those steps, and the partner's page fails. Nobody announced the change, because nobody knew there was a contract to break. Shipping cannot know what else its consumers depend on, so any change may break one of them. Shipping can stop changing, or coordinate every change with every consumer. Either way, the system can no longer be maintained.

A specified contract puts Shipping back in control. What Shipping chooses to expose becomes its public interface, the edge at which consumers connect, and everything else stays private. The contract is a guardrail for change. Inside that boundary, Shipping remains free to evolve. The smaller the interface, the smaller the impact of a change.

## Specify the interface

A specification describes the **operations** a system offers to the outside world: what has to be supplied and what comes back, errors included. Shipping's tracking operation takes a parcel's identifier and returns two fields, a status and an expected delivery date.

The **protocol**, the way the two sides exchange data, is left open. Web requests, messages or files all qualify, as long as the contract is specified. Whether the specification comes first or last is likewise up to the team, which is why we do not call this pillar "contract first".

The specification has two kinds of reader. People should find it as readable as possible, so that producer and consumers can discuss it. Machines should be able to read it too, so that code on both sides can be generated from it.

A specification is also a design tool. To describe what a consumer can do, Shipping's team has to think as the teams behind the frontend and the partner's website do, so names and data are chosen with more care.

## Keep it small

The first decision is how much to expose. Everything a system exposes has to be maintained, so the more it opens up, the harder it becomes to change. The **interface segregation principle**, the I in the SOLID design principles, makes a related point: no consumer should be forced to depend on what it does not use.

Shipping therefore gives away as little as it can, because adding later is easy and taking back is hard. Its consumers ask where a parcel is and when it arrives, and the contract offers that and no more. Which carrier delivers the parcel stays inside, and the seven internal steps appear as three statuses, so merging two steps no longer concerns the partner.

The contract protects in both directions. Shipping does not want its internals to leak out, and the partner does not want Shipping's model pushed into its own. Each side therefore translates between the contract and its own model, which on the consumer's side is known as an **anti-corruption layer**, a guard that keeps a foreign model out. That translation is mapping, and [Domain isolation](/domain-isolation#the-price-is-mapping) explains what it costs.

## Every change is a new version

Even a small contract changes, but slowly, because every change involves parties with plans of their own. That is why an interface deserves careful design: nobody wants to change it often. Versioning records the changes and keeps consumers informed.

Every contract has a version, which labels it rather than forming part of it. A **hash**, a fingerprint computed from the contract's text, is enough. A numbered release serves consumers better, because it marks a deliberate step and tells them what Shipping exposes from now on.

Every change to the contract produces a new version. A **non-breaking** change leaves every existing consumer working, and a **breaking** change forces at least one of them to adapt. Two tests decide which it is. A new version is **backward compatible** when it still accepts everything existing consumers send. It is **forward compatible** when those consumers can cope with everything it returns, which requires that they ignore what they do not recognise, a habit known as the **tolerant reader**.

A new operation or a new optional field passes both tests, so the new version silently replaces the old one and nobody has to act.

A field that is removed, renamed or changed in type fails one of the two tests, and so does a new required input. Shipping then informs its consumers, publishes version 2 beside version 1 and **deprecates** version 1: it still works, but an announcement says when it goes away and what replaces it. Consumers move over before that date, on their own schedule.

Every version left running costs maintenance. Shipping therefore counts the calls each version receives, per consumer, and switching version 1 off becomes a countdown rather than a guess.

## Beyond the contract

A contract, however well versioned, does not hold everything. It holds shapes, and well-chosen names convey intent. Behaviour is hard to capture in a static document, so some things change without the contract changing at all, and consumers break all the same.

Ordering is the typical case. Shipping's contract also lists a customer's shipments. The list has always come back oldest first, only because the database returned it that way, and the frontend takes the last three items as the customer's recent deliveries. Then Shipping moves to another database, and customers see parcels from years ago. No version changed, and something broke.

This is **Hyrum's Law**: with enough consumers, every observable behaviour will be relied on by somebody. Behaviour that matters therefore belongs in the contract, for example as an input that sets the order, with a stated default. A consumer should rely on nothing the contract does not state. A producer should treat a visible change in behaviour as a change to the contract.

## Conclusion

Every system that others connect to has a contract, and the only choice is whether it is specified. A specified contract lives in one central place, readable by people and machines, and carries a version. Kept small, it leaves everything behind it free to change. Every change is a new version. A non-breaking change, compatible backward and forward, is released silently. A breaking change is announced, and old and new run side by side until the old version is switched off. The producer still has to watch over the behaviour that no contract can hold.

Specified contracts are the edge of the circle. [Domain isolation](/domain-isolation) is about what the edge protects, [Event-driven](/event-driven) about what travels from one edge to the next, and the [overview](/) shows how the three depend on each other.
