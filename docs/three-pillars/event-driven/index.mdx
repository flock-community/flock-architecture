# Event-driven

An event is a record of something that happened in the business: a customer moved house, an order was placed. Most software throws that information away: it keeps how things are now, and every change overwrites what was there before. An event-driven system keeps the events. Parts of the system tell each other what happened instead of asking how things are, and the events become the record of the business.

This is the pillar with the widest range: from publishing what happens in one domain so that others can react, to making the events the only thing you store.

## Storing state, or recording what happened

Take the customer's address. In most systems it is a column in a table, updated when the customer moves, and the old address is gone. An event-driven system records the move instead: this customer moved house, to this address, on this date. Nothing is overwritten. The current address follows from the latest move, and where the customer lived last year is still there.

<StateHistoryFigure />

Storing state is enough for a lot of software, but the questions a business asks are mostly about what happened. How many customers moved last quarter? A system that stores state can't answer them, because the update destroyed the information. The idea is not new: an accountant doesn't overwrite the balance, but adds an entry to the ledger, which is trusted because nothing in it is ever lost.

## What makes a good event

An event is a fact, and everything else follows from that.

**It is named after what happened, in the past tense, in the words of the business.** Order placed. Payment received. Parcel handed to carrier, not `ShipmentDispatched`, if that is what the warehouse says. The names of your events are the vocabulary of the system, and a good one lasts for years. If you have run an [event storming](/domain-isolation#event-storming-then-modelling) workshop, its wall of notes in the past tense is the first draft of your events.

**It carries what happened, not everything.** A move carries the customer's identifier, the new address and the date, not the whole customer record. Anything else it refers to [by identifier](/domain-isolation#refer-by-identifier-and-nothing-more).

**It never changes.** A wrong fact is corrected by a new event, the way an accountant reverses an entry instead of erasing it. Anything that can be edited afterwards is state, not history.

**It is not a command.** A command asks for something to happen and can be refused: place this order. An event records that something did happen, and is addressed to nobody in particular. Publish "ship order" as an event, and you are telling other domains what to do.

## Telling instead of asking

Events matter most at the boundary between two domains. When a customer places an order, Shipping has to prepare a shipment. Checkout can call Shipping and ask for one, but then Checkout has to know that Shipping exists and what it needs, and Payments and the warehouse each add one more call. Or Checkout publishes what happened, an order was placed, and Shipping listens. Then listeners come and go without Checkout knowing.

<AskListenFigure />

The difference is who depends on whom. With a call, the domain where something happens depends on every domain that has to react. With an event, the reactions depend on the fact, and the domain that produced it depends on nobody. That is what travels between the circles on the [overview](/): facts, which others can use without reaching inside.

The same mechanism feeds copies. Domain isolation [allows](/domain-isolation#one-source-of-truth-and-copies-you-can-throw-away) Shipping a copy of the delivery address as long as Customers stays the source of truth, and events keep it fresh: Shipping handles every move, and rebuilds the copy from the events when it is wrong.

### A moment later

The reaction happens after the event, so for a moment Checkout knows about an order that Shipping hasn't seen yet. This is called **eventual consistency**: every part arrives at the same picture, but not at the same instant.

Sounds like a weakness? The business has always worked this way: the warehouse picks the parcel hours after the order is placed, and nobody calls that an inconsistency. Ask at each boundary how long the business can live with the gap. Where the answer is "not at all", the two belong in one domain, because [a transaction never spans two domains](/domain-isolation#one-transaction-one-domain).

### Making sure it arrives

An event that is published and lost is worse than no event, because the publisher believes the rest of the system knows. So write the event to the database in the same transaction as the change, and let a separate process publish it from there, the **outbox pattern**. A publisher that is unsure retries, so a listener has to be **idempotent**: handling an event twice has the same result as handling it once. And keep everything that must stay in order in one stream, such as one order's events.

## Keeping the events

So far the events have been messages that could be thrown away once everyone has read them. The next step is to keep every event, permanently, in the order it happened. The events become the record, and everything else a view of that record.

Any table can then be rebuilt from them. The order overview, the sales per day and the search index are each a different reading of the same events. Such a table is a **projection**: a shape of the data made for one kind of question, and disposable.

<ViewsFigure />

When the business wants a report it never asked for, you write the projection and let it read the events from the beginning, so the report is complete on the day it is built. And the audit trail is free, because the primary record is a log.

### The source of truth

The last step is to make the events the only thing a domain writes. To handle a command, the domain replays the events of that order, decides whether the command is allowed, and if so appends new events. The state is never stored, only derived. This is **event sourcing**, and nothing can change without an event saying so.

Old events never go away, and that is easy to underestimate. The event written in the first year will still be read in the tenth, in whatever shape it had then. An event that leaves a domain is a contract, and everything about versioning in [Specified contracts](/specified-contracts) applies to it.

Event sourcing is a choice per domain, not per system. It pays off where the history is the business: orders, payments, anything that gets disputed. It is overkill for a table of countries.

## What it asks of a team

Event-driven is a way of thinking before it is a way of building. "What do I call to make this happen" gives way to "what happened, and who needs to know". Names get the care of a public interface from day one, because renaming a published event is a migration. Decide what to do with personal data before the first event with a name in it is written, because the right to be forgotten doesn't sit well with a record that cannot be edited. And you don't need new infrastructure to start: a table of events in the database you already have is a fine first event-driven system.

## When request-and-response is the right choice

Not everything is an event. "Is this card valid?" is a question that needs an answer now: a request, answered by the domain that owns the data. The choice is how far to go in each part, not between two kinds of system. Our advice: publish the events of every domain, in the past tense and in the words of the business, and keep them. That step is cheap, and expensive to take later, because the history you didn't record is gone.

## Conclusion

An event is a fact: something that happened, named in the past tense and in the words of the business. Record what happened, and the current state follows from it, along with the answers to questions nobody has asked yet. Domains tell instead of ask: a domain publishes what happened and doesn't know who listens. Keep the events, and every table becomes a view you can throw away and rebuild. Make them the source of truth, and nothing changes without a record. How far you take it is a choice per domain.

Events are what travels between the circles. [Domain isolation](/domain-isolation) is about what is inside a circle, [Specified contracts](/specified-contracts) about its edge, and the [overview](/) shows how the three depend on each other.
